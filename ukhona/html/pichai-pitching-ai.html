Two Months After Calling the AI Investment Boom "Irrational," Sundar Pichai Now Defends It — "I Think the Investment Makes Sense"

At India's AI Impact Summit, the Google CEO reversed course, comparing today's AI buildout to railroads and highways — and projecting an industrial revolution happening ten times faster.

📰 Times of India Tech Desk 📅 February 19, 2026 🏙 New Delhi ⏱ 4 min read
SP

"This is the industrial revolution, but 10 times faster and 10 times larger."

— Sundar Pichai, CEO, Alphabet / Google  ·  India AI Impact Summit, February 2026

Sundar Pichai speaking at a media briefing on the sidelines of the India AI Impact Summit, New Delhi.  ·  PTI / Deccan Chronicle

Amid swelling global fears of an AI investment bubble, Google CEO Sundar Pichai stepped onto the stage in New Delhi this week and did something unexpected: he defended the very spending spree he had, just two months prior, described as carrying "elements of irrationality." At a media briefing on the sidelines of the India AI Impact Summit, Pichai urged boardrooms worldwide to take a long view — and compared today's AI infrastructure build-out to the foundational bets that once built America's railroads and interstate highways.

"I think we are investing to meet that moment," Pichai said, fielding questions on how CEOs can overcome boardroom scepticism about AI returns. "In some of the contexts Dennis has spoken about — this is the industrial revolution, but 10 times faster and 10 times larger." Dennis referred to Demis Hassabis, CEO of Google DeepMind, who had addressed the summit earlier in the day.

These are such leverage investments and drive so much growth and value. The investment makes sense given the economy we are seeing and the opportunities we see.

— Sundar Pichai, CEO, Alphabet · India AI Impact Summit, Feb 19, 2026

The remarks mark a striking tonal shift from Pichai's November 2025 interview with the BBC, in which he acknowledged that the AI investment surge had "elements of irrationality" and warned that no company — "including us" — would be immune if the AI bubble were to burst. That interview drew widespread attention for its candour, with comparisons to former US Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan's famous 1996 warning of "irrational exuberance" in the stock market ahead of the dot-com crash.

The context for the reversal, or nuancing, is significant. Alphabet's plan to devote between \$175 billion and \$185 billion to capital expenditure in 2026 — announced weeks before the summit — would represent a dramatic escalation at a moment when rivals Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta are all engaged in similarly staggering AI infrastructure spending. Four years ago, Google was spending less than \$30 billion annually on AI. This year, it will spend over \$90 billion. Next year, potentially twice that.

Pichai pointed to surging demand for Google Cloud as concrete evidence that returns are already materialising. The AI buildout, he argued, is not speculative excess but a response to real, compounding demand — and that the infrastructure being laid today will prove as indispensable as the physical infrastructure of previous industrial revolutions.

"And so we are investing to meet that demand," Pichai said. "And you know, that's what makes this an exciting moment. So I think, the investment makes sense given the progress in the technology we are seeing, and the opportunities we see on top of it."

The tech industry remains at a contentious crossroads. Staggering capital expenditure — Alphabet's $185 billion commitment for 2026 being a case in point — continues to collide with intensifying fears of an AI bubble, particularly from global financial institutions including the UK's central bank and the International Monetary Fund, both of which issued warnings in late 2025 about the risk of a sharp market correction. For now, Pichai is betting the bubble is not a bubble at all — just history, accelerating.
