The Audit Framework

How We Score Each Tool:
Against Dr. Nair's Actual Problem

Session I documented five specific failures in Dr. Nair's review: 37 hours of automatable work, English-only bias, grey literature exclusion, evidence absence misread as absence of evidence, and no externalized epistemic priors. Every tool gets scored on four dimensions derived from these failures.

DimensionWhat It MeasuresWhy It Matters for WHO-India
Time RecoveryHours saved from Dr. Nair's 37 automatable hrsOfficers have 3 weeks and multiple workstreams
India FitAbility to surface India-specific, non-NEJM sourcesMost tools trained on Western biomedical corpus
Grey LitHandles CPCB, ICMR, state-level reports, preprintsBest Tier-2 city data is outside PubMed
Gap-MappingExplicitly identifies what's missing, not just what's foundPolicy brief depends on gap identification

Each dimension scored 1–5. A tool scoring below 10/20 overall is not recommended for WHO-India's current workflow without significant prompt engineering.

Tool-by-Tool Assessment

Ten Tools, Honestly Scored

El
Elicit
elicit.org · Subscription · ~$10–50/month
▲ Strong Fit — Primary Tool

What it does: Elicit automates the most painful part of Dr. Nair's workflow — data extraction. Upload a set of PDFs or paste DOIs; Elicit reads them and populates a structured table: study design, sample size, population, intervention, outcome, effect estimate. It also runs searches across a large academic corpus and summarizes findings per paper.

The honest limitation: Elicit's corpus skews toward English-language indexed literature. It will not find the ICMR 2021 UP/Bihar respiratory report, nor State pollution board grey literature. It also sometimes hallucinate numeric values — a 10% random validation check on extracted data is non-negotiable.

Applied to Dr. Nair's Review
Days 4–7 (data extraction, 30 hrs) → reduced to ~12 hrs. Elicit handles the structural extraction; Dr. Nair reviews and validates. Net recovery: ~18 hours. Days 1–3 search strategy: Elicit helps iterate search strings faster — saves ~6 additional hours. Total potential recovery: 24 of 37 automatable hours.

Cl
Claude (Anthropic)
claude.ai · Free tier available · Pro $20/month
▲ Strong Fit — Document Analysis

What it does: Claude's 200,000-token context window means Dr. Nair can upload an entire CPCB annual report, an ICMR grey literature document, or a 50-page WHO IRIS publication and ask specific questions about it. It synthesizes, compares, and — critically — can be prompted to identify what is absent from a document, not just what's present.

The honest limitation: Claude does not search the internet by default (though web search is available). It works on documents you bring to it. It is not a replacement for Elicit's systematic search; it is a complement for deep reading of complex documents. It can also be prompted to adopt WHO-India-specific epistemic priors — which is the foundation of Session III's framework.

Applied to Dr. Nair's Review
The Tier-2 city problem: Dr. Nair found a CPCB 2023 annual air quality report but couldn't read all 340 pages in 3 weeks. Claude reads it in seconds, extracts Patna/Kanpur/Varanasi data, identifies what's missing. Gap-mapping for the intervention section: prompt Claude to list what a complete evidence base would contain, then compare against what was found. This is the "evidence absence ≠ absence of evidence" move.

AS
ASReview
asreview.nl · Open source · Free
▲ Strong Fit — Screening

What it does: ASReview uses active machine learning to prioritize which titles and abstracts to screen. You label a small seed set (relevant/irrelevant), and the model learns your criteria and surfaces the most likely-relevant papers first. It dramatically reduces the time to screen large result sets while maintaining systematic review standards.

The honest limitation: There's a learning curve — setup requires some technical comfort. It doesn't help with the retrieval itself, only the screening step. And its effectiveness depends on a good initial seed set, which requires domain expertise.

Applied to Dr. Nair's Review
Days 1–3 title/abstract screening (~400 papers): ASReview reduces this to ~2 hours of active screening with the model handling prioritization. The model learns "ASHA-deliverable interventions, under-5, India" from Dr. Nair's early labels and surfaces the 53 relevant papers without requiring her to read all 400. Recovery: ~15 hours in the screening phase alone.

Consensus
consensus.app · Free tier · Pro ~$9/month
◆ Partial Fit — Quick Queries

What it does: Consensus searches a large academic corpus and returns a "Consensus Meter" — a visual indicator of what the literature broadly says about a question — plus bullet-point summaries with citations. Excellent for answering bounded questions quickly: "Does PM2.5 exposure affect lung development in children?"

The honest limitation: Corpus is heavily PubMed-weighted and Anglophone. The Consensus Meter is genuinely useful but can create false confidence when the apparent consensus is actually a publication bias artifact. For India-specific queries, results skew Tier-1 city and clinical rather than public health intervention.

Applied to Dr. Nair's Review
Useful for quickly answering: "What does the literature say about PM2.5 and under-5 respiratory outcomes?" — gets Dr. Nair a solid starting framework in 20 minutes. Not useful for: finding ASHA-specific interventions, Tier-2 city evidence, or grey literature. Best used at the start of the review to orient, not as a primary retrieval tool.

OE
OpenEvidence
openevidence.com · Free for clinicians
▼ Poor Fit — Wrong Domain

What it does: OpenEvidence queries NEJM and JAMA APIs to provide low-hallucination clinical answers. It is genuinely excellent for what it does: clinical medicine, drug therapy, diagnostic criteria. Its strength is citation discipline and low confabulation rate.

The honest limitation for WHO-India public health work: NEJM and JAMA publish almost no research on ASHA-delivered community interventions in Tier-2 Indian cities. Its strength — tight database curation — is precisely its failure mode for this use case. Asking OpenEvidence about PM2.5 interventions for Bihar is asking a Harvard cardiologist about village water sanitation. Excellent expertise, entirely wrong terrain.

Applied to Dr. Nair's Review
Not recommended. Would return high-quality evidence from Western urban settings with near-zero India-specific content. Could actively mislead by providing confident answers from the wrong population. Use only if Dr. Nair needs to check a specific pharmacological or clinical question that the brief touches on tangentially.

Rayyan
rayyan.ai · Free for individuals · Team plans available
▲ Strong Fit — Collaborative Reviews

What it does: Rayyan is the industry standard for systematic review screening collaboration. Import citations, assign to reviewers, enable blinded dual-screening, flag conflicts, resolve disagreements. Its AI layer suggests relevance based on learned criteria and highlights key terms in abstracts.

The honest limitation: Rayyan is most valuable for multi-reviewer systematic reviews. For Dr. Nair's rapid synthesis (one primary reviewer), it's somewhat over-engineered. However, for WHO-India team reviews involving SEARO collaboration, it becomes essential.

Applied to Dr. Nair's Review
For this specific rapid review: useful but not critical — ASReview covers the single-reviewer screening need better. For the next WHO-India systematic review involving Dr. Mehta and SEARO colleagues: Rayyan is the correct tool. Recommend installing and training on it now so it's ready when a team review arrives.

The Integrated Workflow

How the Tools Work Together:
Dr. Nair's 17 Days, Revised

No single tool solves the problem. The power comes from sequencing them correctly — each tool handling the phase it's genuinely suited for, and handing off cleanly to the next. Below is the revised workflow for a WHO-India rapid synthesis.

▸ Revised Rapid Synthesis Workflow — AI-Augmented
1
Consensus + Claude
Orient to landscape. Define scope. Draft search strategy. Day 1 only.
2
PubMed + WHO IRIS + SEARO Index
Systematic retrieval across expanded databases. Days 1–2.
3
ASReview
ML-prioritized title/abstract screening. 400 → 53 papers in 2 hours. Day 2.
4
Elicit + Claude
Data extraction (Elicit) + grey literature deep reading (Claude). Days 3–6.
5
Claude + Officer
GRADE assessment, gap-mapping, narrative synthesis, policy brief. Days 7–12.

The 50-hour recovery is not the point. The point is what Dr. Nair does with those 50 hours: deeper engagement with the grey literature that no AI can read for her; a proper second opinion on the GRADE assessments; time to write a genuine recommendations section rather than a hasty one; bandwidth to call the SEARO colleagues who would have flagged the missing AYUSH evidence. The tools do not make the review better. They make the officer better by giving her time to be one.

The Honest Ceiling

What No Current AI Tool
Can Do for WHO-India

Having surveyed the tools enthusiastically, it is essential to name what they cannot do — because these gaps are precisely what Session III will address.

GapWhy AI Tools Fail HereWhat's Actually Needed
Vernacular health literatureAll major tools train on English-indexed corpora. Hindi, Bengali, Telugu public health research is structurally invisible.A retrieval layer that explicitly queries non-English sources and weights them appropriately
ASHA-specific intervention evidenceThis evidence barely exists in indexed literature. AI cannot synthesize what wasn't published. It can only flag the absence more clearly.Research agenda advocacy — which is itself a legitimate review output
Contextual judgmentNo tool knows that a 2019 RCT from Pune is inapplicable to rural Bihar without being told explicitly why.Officer expertise + explicit epistemic priors baked into the prompt
Non-stationarityTools don't know that India's air quality landscape changed significantly post-2020 COVID lockdowns, or post-NCAP implementation. They return old and new evidence with equal weight.Explicit date-weighting and policy-change awareness in the retrieval protocol
Institutional accountabilityNo AI tool produces a review that a WHO clearance committee can audit. The process, decisions, and exclusion rationale must still be human-legible.AI as documentation assistant, not decision-maker
The tools we have today are excellent at reducing the cost of the wrong kind of work — the mechanical, the repetitive, the well-defined. They are genuinely poor at reducing the cost of the right kind of work — the contextual, the political, the terrain-specific. That gap is not a failure of AI. It is an instruction about what human expertise is actually for.

Session III introduces the Ukubona framework — a way of baking WHO-India's specific epistemic priors directly into the AI retrieval process, addressing the contextual judgment problem at its root. Session IV turns that framework into a deployable protocol. But neither is possible without the honest tool inventory conducted here.
