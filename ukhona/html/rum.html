I. What Is Rum?

User what is a rum?

In its simplest terms, rum is a distilled alcoholic spirit made from sugarcane or its byproducts. Unlike many other spirits, it has no single global set of production rules, making it one of the most diverse and versatile categories of liquor in the world.

Core Ingredients

Rum must be made from 100% sugarcane products. The specific source significantly influences the final flavor:

  • Molasses: A thick, dark syrup left over from sugar refining. Most rums (~95%) are made from molasses, which typically produces rich, fruity, and caramel-like flavors.
  • Sugarcane Juice: Freshly pressed juice used primarily for Rhum Agricole (French-style) and Cachaça (Brazilian). These are known for "grassy," earthy, and floral profiles.
  • Sugarcane Syrup: Sometimes called "cane honey," this is juice that has been boiled down but not yet refined into sugar.
Common Types

Because "color" can be manipulated with additives like caramel or removed via charcoal filtration, it isn't always a reliable indicator of age.

  • White (Silver/Light) Rum: Usually light-bodied and clear. Often used as a neutral base for cocktails like Mojitos and Daiquiris.
  • Gold (Amber) Rum: Typically aged in wooden barrels (often ex-bourbon casks) to gain color and a smoother, vanilla-tinted flavor.
  • Dark (Black) Rum: Often aged longer in heavily charred barrels or enriched with extra molasses/caramel. These are bold and frequently used in "Tiki" drinks or for cooking.
  • Spiced & Flavored Rum: Infused with botanicals (cinnamon, vanilla, citrus peel) or fruit extracts (coconut, pineapple).
  • Overproof Rum: High-alcohol spirits (usually 50%–80% ABV) used for potent punches or lighting drinks on fire.
History & Origin
  • Birthplace: While sugarcane-based ferments have existed for thousands of years in Asia, modern rum distillation as we know it originated in the Caribbean during the 17th century.
  • Legacy: It was famously associated with the British Royal Navy, where sailors were given a daily "tot" (ration) of rum until 1970. Its history is also deeply intertwined with the triangular trade and colonial sugar plantations.
  • Etymology: The word "rum" likely comes from "rumbullion" or "rumbustion," 17th-century slang for a "great tumult" or "uproar."

While people were fermenting sugarcane juice in Asia and India for millennia, the 1600s changed everything. On Caribbean sugar plantations (specifically Barbados), enslaved people discovered that molasses—the sticky, "garbage" byproduct of sugar production—could be fermented into alcohol.

The "Kill-Devil"

Early rum was so harsh and potent it was literally called "Kill-Devil" or "Rumbullion". It was cheap, strong, and used to "drown the sorrows" of a very brutal era.

The Royal Navy

By 1655, the British Navy swapped their sailors' daily beer ration for rum because beer spoiled on long voyages, but rum actually "mellowed" in the barrels. This led to the famous "Daily Tot" tradition that lasted until 1970.

Economic Fuel

Rum became a "liquid currency." It was a cornerstone of the Triangular Trade, where it was traded for enslaved people in Africa, who were then brought to the Caribbean to produce the sugar/molasses to make more rum.

American Revolution

Before whiskey took over, rum was the most popular drink in Colonial America. According to the Mount Vernon estate records, even George Washington insisted on a barrel of high-quality Barbados rum for his inauguration.

It went from being a "trash" byproduct of the sugar industry to the spirit that literally fueled empires and navies.
