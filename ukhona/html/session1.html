01
The Architecture of Authority

Chain of Command:
Where a Literature Review Begins

A literature review does not begin with a researcher opening PubMed. It begins weeks, sometimes months earlier, in a room where no researcher is present — where a disease burden is named, a gap is identified, a political window is sensed. Understanding this is the first discipline of public health evidence work.

In the WHO-India system, the evidence chain descends through distinct institutional layers before it reaches the officer who will actually conduct the review. Each layer shapes what question gets asked — and, quietly, what answers are permissible.

1
WHO Global Headquarters — Geneva

A resolution is passed at the World Health Assembly, or an internal technical advisory group flags a priority area. Global health targets are set — e.g., the SDG 3.9 commitment to reducing deaths from air pollution. This is the frame that will eventually determine what counts as "relevant" evidence six levels below.

The loss function is being defined here. Not by officers. Not by evidence. By politics and negotiation.
2
WHO South-East Asia Regional Office (SEARO) — New Delhi

SEARO translates global mandates into regional priorities. The Regional Director's office identifies India's burden — in this case, that 14 of the world's 20 most polluted cities are Indian, and that under-5 respiratory mortality in Delhi, Patna, and Kanpur represents the largest preventable burden in the region.

The terrain becomes specific. But still no officer is touching a database.
3
WHO-India Country Office — Technical Cluster

The Programme Director (Environmental Health) receives a tasking from SEARO. She identifies which technical officer has the domain knowledge, bandwidth, and seniority to lead the synthesis. She writes the mandate memo — the document Dr. Priya Nair will receive.

First time a human being picks a topic. Still above the officer level.
4
Technical Officer — Dr. Priya Nair ← We begin here

Dr. Nair acknowledges the mandate. She has three weeks. She is also managing two other workstreams. The review she will produce will inform a policy brief that will inform SEARO's recommendations that will inform India's national action plan on air quality. She opens her laptop. She opens PubMed.

This is the moment the gap between the terrain (Indian cities, ASHA workers, Tier-2 hospitals) and the map (English-language PubMed results) begins to open.
5
Peer Review & Clearance

Dr. Nair's draft goes to an internal technical reviewer, then to the Programme Director, then for legal clearance. Any recommendation touching pharmaceutical intervention goes through an additional SEARO review. Time elapsed: typically 4–8 weeks post-submission.

The evidence landscape can change significantly during this period. A study published after submission cannot be included.
6
Policy Brief → National Uptake

The cleared synthesis becomes a WHO-India policy brief. It is shared with the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, the Central Pollution Control Board, and State Health Departments. Recommendations are absorbed (or quietly set aside) into India's National Clean Air Programme and the National Health Mission.

The literature review has now become policy. Its limitations have traveled with it, invisible.

The core observation: By the time an officer opens PubMed, the question has already been substantially shaped by negotiation, political timing, institutional priorities, and the linguistic assumptions of whoever wrote the mandate. The literature review is not step one. It is step four — arriving into a frame it did not choose.

Simulated Internal Communication

The Mandate Document
as Dr. Nair received it

The following is a simulated WHO-India internal memo — the kind of document that initiates a literature review. Read it carefully. Notice what it specifies and, more importantly, what it leaves unspecified. These silences will shape every search term Dr. Nair types.

WORLD HEALTH ORGANIZATION
India Country Office · Environmental Health Cluster
C/5-B, Safdarjung Development Area · New Delhi 110 016

REF: WHO/IND/EH/2026/014
DATE: 3 February 2026
TO: Dr. Priya Nair, Technical Officer (Environmental Health)
FROM: Dr. Ramesh Bhatt, Programme Director (Environmental Health)
CC: Dr. Anand Krishnamurthy, WHO Representative, India
Internal · Routine
Subject
Tasking for Rapid Evidence Synthesis: Ambient Air Pollution and Under-5 Respiratory Outcomes in Indian Urban Settings

Dr. Nair,

Following the SEARO Environmental Health Technical Advisory Group meeting of 22 January 2026, this office has been tasked with providing an evidence brief on the above subject for inclusion in SEARO's Regional Framework for Air Quality and Health (2026–2030).

You are requested to lead the preparation of a rapid evidence synthesis addressing the following:

Scope
Epidemiological evidence on associations between PM2.5/PM10 exposure and respiratory outcomes in children under five years in Tier-1 and Tier-2 Indian cities (2010–2025)
Interventions
Community-level interventions deliverable through existing ASHA/ASHA-facilitator network; household air quality measures; early identification protocols at primary care level
Gaps
Identification of evidence gaps for potential prioritization in WHO-India's 2026 research agenda
Format
Narrative synthesis with evidence tables; GRADE assessment of key recommendations where feasible; 12–15 pages excluding annexes
Deadline
27 February 2026 (first draft for internal review)
Resources
WHO Library access; HINARI subscription; institutional PubMed account; existing CPCB datasets on file with EH cluster

Please acknowledge receipt and flag immediately if bandwidth constraints exist. SEARO deadline is not flexible.

With regards,

Dr. Ramesh Bhatt
Programme Director, Environmental Health
WHO India Country Office
+ 91 11 4600 1000
Analysis — What the mandate doesn't say
Notice what is absent: no mention of vernacular literature (Hindi, Bengali, Telugu health studies), no mention of AYUSH primary care data, no explicit guidance on weighting grey literature (CPCB annual reports, State pollution boards), and critically — no instruction on what to do when the ASHA-deliverable intervention evidence simply does not exist in indexed databases. These silences are not oversight. They are structural.

Simulated Working Session · 9:15 AM · WHO-India Office, New Delhi

Dr. Nair Begins:
The Review as It Is Done Today

What follows is a faithful simulation of how a WHO technical officer currently conducts a rapid evidence synthesis. The dialogue includes Dr. Nair's thinking process, her interactions with colleagues, and the decision points where friction accumulates. We play this in real time.

Simulation · Day 1 of 17 · 09:15 hrs WHO-India · New Delhi Office
Dr. Nair
Technical Officer
Environ. Health
[Opens email. Reads mandate. Types acknowledgment. Opens a new Word document. Types the title: "Rapid Evidence Synthesis — Ambient Air Pollution and Under-5 Respiratory Outcomes in Indian Urban Settings." Stares at it for a moment. Opens PubMed.]

Right. Air pollution, children, India. ASHA delivery. Let me start broad and narrow down. Search one: "PM2.5 children respiratory India." [returns 4,847 results] That's not useful. Let me add date filter — 2015 to 2025. [2,341 results]. Still too many. Add "urban" — [1,102]. Add "intervention" — [287]. That's workable.
Dr. Nair
Internal monologue
[Scanning titles. First page of results: 8 from the US, 3 from China, 2 from Europe, 1 from India — a 2022 study from AIIMS Delhi on traffic-related air pollution in school children. Clicks it. Reads abstract. The study measures PM2.5 at school gates in South Delhi. Sample: middle-class urban children. Intervention: none. Outcome: spirometry measurements.]

This is relevant for epidemiology but not for interventions. And South Delhi isn't representative of Tier-2 cities. Make a note. [Types a note into Word: "Limited India-specific intervention RCTs — mostly observational epidemiology from Tier-1 cities."]
Dr. Mehta
Colleague
Epidemiology desk
[Stops by Dr. Nair's desk]

Priya, are you doing the air quality review? Did you know there's a Lancet Planetary Health piece from last year — they did a meta-analysis on South Asian cities? It's quite good. I can send you the DOI.
Dr. Nair
Response
Please, yes. [Receives DOI. Opens paper. South Asian meta-analysis — 23 studies, of which 6 are from India. Interventions section: "Further research required." Adds to evidence table. Notes: "No ASHA-deliverable intervention found in this meta-analysis."]

[Now searches specifically for "ASHA community health worker air pollution intervention India." Returns: 12 results. Of which 3 are relevant. None are randomized trials. All are feasibility assessments from 2017–2019.]
Dr. Nair
Day 3 · 11:40 hrs
[Three days in. The evidence table now has 34 papers. Of these: 26 are epidemiological (exposure-outcome associations), 5 are intervention studies (none ASHA-specific), 3 are systematic reviews. The Tier-2 city evidence is particularly thin — most studies are from Delhi, Mumbai, and Bengaluru. Patna, Kanpur, Varanasi — the cities with highest burden — are nearly absent from the indexed literature.]

There's a real problem here. CPCB publishes annual air quality data for all these cities. There's a ICMR report from 2021 on respiratory burden in UP and Bihar that's grey literature — not indexed. Do I include it? The mandate says "evidence synthesis" but doesn't define evidence. Technically I should use GRADE criteria. Grey literature would be low GRADE quality. But it's the only data on Patna...
Dr. Bhatt
Programme Director
(check-in call)
Priya, how are we doing? SEARO is asking for an early update on what we expect to recommend.
Dr. Nair
Response
Sir, the epidemiological evidence is strong — PM2.5 is clearly associated with under-5 lung outcomes. The problem is interventions. There is essentially no high-quality evidence for ASHA-delivered air quality interventions. The best evidence base is from household biomass combustion reduction in rural settings — LPG substitution — but the mandate is asking about urban Tier-2 settings where the exposure source is traffic and industry, not cooking.

I can recommend surveillance strengthening and a research agenda. I can flag the gap. But I cannot synthesize evidence that doesn't exist in the indexed literature.
Dr. Bhatt
Response
I understand. Do your best. SEARO knows the evidence is thin in this area — that's partly why they asked for the review. Write clearly about the gap. The brief can advocate for research funding as its primary output.
Observer's Note — The Gap Becomes Visible
Dr. Nair has now spent three days and produced a technically sound preliminary evidence table. But notice what has already happened: she is working entirely within the English-language PubMed-indexed corpus. The ICMR grey literature, the State pollution board reports, the Patna-specific clinical data from district hospitals — none of this is in the standard retrieval architecture. The gap between what the mandate needs (evidence for ASHA delivery in Tier-2 cities) and what the search engine surfaces (South Delhi spirometry studies and US-funded interventions) is structural, not personal. Dr. Nair is not failing. The system is performing exactly as designed — for a different territory.

Process Anatomy

The Seventeen Days:
A Timeline of the Review

To understand where AI assistance can be transformative, we must first understand exactly where time is spent in the existing process. Below is a faithful reconstruction of Dr. Nair's seventeen working days.

Days 1–3
Search Strategy & Initial Retrieval
Manual iteration through 15+ search string variants across PubMed, Cochrane Library, and WHO IRIS. Deduplication by hand across databases. Initial screening of ~400 titles/abstracts for relevance. Time spent: ~20 hours. Irreplaceable human judgment: ~3 hours.
Days 4–7
Full-Text Retrieval & Data Extraction
Full-text access for 67 papers. 14 unavailable through HINARI — requested via interlibrary loan, 6 returned within the window. Reading and extracting data into evidence table: sample size, study design, exposure measure, outcome, population demographics, intervention description, effect estimate. Time spent: ~30 hours. Irreplaceable human judgment: ~25 hours.
Days 8–10
GRADE Assessment & Evidence Synthesis
Quality assessment of 53 included papers using GRADE methodology. Discussion with Dr. Mehta on two contested papers. Drafting narrative synthesis sections. The intervention evidence section takes two full days because the absence of evidence must be written as carefully as the evidence itself. Time spent: ~24 hours.
Days 11–14
Drafting, Revision & Internal Review
Full draft produced at Day 11. Internal review by Dr. Bhatt returns on Day 13 with 23 comments — mostly requests for clearer framing of the intervention gap and more specific research recommendations. Revision takes two days. Time spent: ~18 hours.
Days 15–17
Clearance, Formatting & Submission
WHO-India formatting requirements: specific reference style, executive summary format, SEARO cover page, annex structure. Technical review by a second officer. Legal clearance check (no issues). Final submission. Time spent: ~8 hours. Irreplaceable human judgment: ~0 hours.
Phase Total Hours Human Judgment Required AI-Automatable
Search Strategy 20 hrs 3 hrs (defining scope) ~85%
Data Extraction 30 hrs 25 hrs (interpretation) ~30%
GRADE & Synthesis 24 hrs 20 hrs (expert judgment) ~15%
Drafting & Revision 18 hrs 15 hrs (argumentation) ~20%
Clearance & Format 8 hrs 0 hrs ~95%
Total 100 hrs 63 hrs ~40% overall

The insight this table reveals: roughly 37 hours of Dr. Nair's 17-day effort are automatable — not because machines can think, but because they can search, deduplicate, extract structured data, and format faster and without fatigue. Those 37 hours recovered are 37 hours she could spend on the 63 hours of irreplaceable expert judgment that currently get compressed and rushed.

05
The Loss Landscape of the Existing Process

Where the Evidence
Gets Lost

Every step in Dr. Nair's process introduced a loss — a distance between the evidence that exists in the world and the evidence that made it into her synthesis. These losses compound. By the time the policy brief reaches SEARO, the evidence has traveled through five filters, none of which are explicitly labeled.

▸ Cumulative Evidence Loss — Dr. Nair's Review
Language filter (English-only)
0.70
Database filter (PubMed/Cochrane only)
0.55
Geography bias (Tier-1 over Tier-2)
0.65
Study design filter (grey literature)
0.60
Recency (pre-2015 evidence excluded)
0.30
Overall Evidence Loss
0.58

A loss score of 0.58 means that, by conservative estimate, more than half of the evidence relevant to WHO-India's specific question — ASHA-delivered interventions in Tier-2 Indian cities — did not reach Dr. Nair's synthesis. Not because she did poor work. Because the search infrastructure she had access to was built for a different population, a different language, and a different epistemic tradition.

The deepest problem is that the missing evidence is invisible. The policy brief will not say "we could not find evidence for Patna." It will say "evidence is limited." These are not the same statement.

This is the baseline we carry into Session II. Not as indictment of Dr. Nair or of WHO-India's processes — which are, within their constraints, excellent — but as honest cartography of the terrain. You cannot navigate from a map that does not acknowledge its own blank spaces.

Looking Forward

What We Now Know
— and What Comes Next

You have just completed Session I. You now have a precise, operational understanding of the existing literature review process — its chain of command, its decision points, its compounding losses, and the gap between what it needs and what it retrieves.

This understanding is not merely academic. It is the foundation from which Session II can be genuinely useful, because we now know what we are asking AI tools to solve:

Identified Problem What We Need Session
37 hours of automatable work consuming expert time Search automation, deduplication, data extraction tools II
